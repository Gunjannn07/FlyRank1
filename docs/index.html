Abstract

This study asks which content pages under-capture search clicks and deserve review first. It uses an anonymized 30,000-page, 32-client slice with 90-day search and engagement signals, filtered to 16,726 pages from 28 clients that have at least 500 impressions and a valid position. Observed click-through rate (CTR) is compared with the expected CTR for each page’s position group, and a Decision Tree is compared with two simple baselines on client-grouped splits. The Decision Tree did not reliably beat the position-tier baseline, so a transparent opportunity score drives a ranked review queue with reason codes and suggested actions. The output is observational decision-support for editorial prioritization and does not establish causal effects.

1. Introduction and problem statement

Editors have limited time and far more pages than they can inspect. A page that ranks well and is seen often, but is rarely clicked, may need a closer look at its title, snippet or intent match. A page that is rarely seen at all usually does not.

The decision this work supports is simple: which content should be reviewed first. A useful output is a ranked queue with an opportunity score, plain reason codes for why a page is on the list, and a suggested next action. Comparing raw CTR across pages is misleading, because a page at position 2 always earns more clicks than a page at position 9. The score therefore compares each page with other pages in the same position group.

This project does not try to uncover how Google ranks pages, and it does not claim that updating a page causes better performance.

2. Data

Results use the anonymized FlyRank ML Internship starter file content_refresh_anonymized.csv: one row per page, 30,000 pages, 44 columns, 32 pseudonymized clients. All activity totals cover a trailing 90-day window ending at export time. A persistence check compares the most recent 30 days with the 30 days before. Earlier course work inspected the warehouse table fact_content_daily_performance (78,835,655 rows; 9,841,378 for March 2026) for context, but every number in this paper comes from the 30,000-row slice.

What was excluded, and why

  • No position data. An avg_position of 0 means “no position data,” not position zero. All 1,205 such rows were removed, because they would otherwise be mixed into the top position group.
  • Tiny pages. Pages with fewer than 500 impressions have noisy CTR; for example, the highest raw CTRs in the slice come from pages with one impression and one click (100%).
  • Identifying information. No client names, domains, URLs, private queries or raw exports are used. Client and page identifiers are pseudonyms, used only to group data for validation, never as features.

CTR in this dataset is already a percentage (0.76 means 0.76%). Keyword and length fields are missing along content-type lines (for example, one content type has no keyword data), so missing values were handled with median imputation plus “was missing” flags rather than filling with zero.

3. Methodology

Expected CTR and the opportunity score

Pages are placed into four groups by average position: 1–3, 4–10, 11–20 and 21+. A page’s expected CTR is the median CTR of its group. Its missed clicks are the clicks it would have received at that expected CTR, minus its actual clicks, floored at zero. Pages are ranked by missed clicks, so large pages with a large gap rise to the top.

Target, features and baselines

For the model comparison the target is observed CTR (percent). There is no hand-made label. Features are average position, search volume, keyword competition, cost-per-click, word count, character count, content type and search intent. Clicks, impression counts, trend_direction, trend_pct and all identifiers are left out of the features, and a code check stops the run if CTR, clicks, trend fields or identifiers slip in. Three predictors are compared on the same data and metric:

  1. Baseline 1: the global median CTR for every page.
  2. Baseline 2: the median CTR of the page’s position group, learned from training clients only.
  3. Decision Tree: depth 4, at least 100 pages per leaf, seed 42.

Validation design and leakage checks

Every comparison is grouped by client: no client appears in both training and test data. The main evaluation holds out 6 clients (1,260 pages) and trains on 22 (15,466 pages). A 5-fold grouped cross-validation checks how much the result depends on which clients are held out. Success is measured by mean absolute error (MAE, in CTR percentage points; lower is better), R², and Spearman rank correlation, which matters most for a ranked queue.

Because CTR is itself clicks divided by impressions, those columns were kept out of the features so the model must learn from context, not recompute the target.

Do the gaps persist?

As a leakage-safe sanity check, pages were compared with their position group separately in the older and newer 30-day windows (10,944 pages with at least 300 impressions in both). The two gaps are correlated (Spearman 0.60), and 83% of the worst-performing 10% in the older window still sit above the median gap in the newer one. The gaps are therefore not just random noise. This is still an observational pattern.

5. Limitations and honest framing

How to read this paperAll findings are observed, directional decision-support. They show where clicks were lower than comparable pages, not why, and not what would happen after a change.

  • Observational only. One 90-day slice of 30,000 pages. Nothing here shows that rewriting a page improves CTR.
  • Low-CTR slice. CTR is low overall, and top-3 CTR here is far below the figure in the dataset’s own documentation, so these group values should not be generalized.
  • Sensitive to choices. Results depend on the 500-impression floor and on the four position groups.
  • Approximate positions. avg_position is a 90-day average, so position groups are approximate.
  • Systematic missing data. Keyword and length fields are missing along content-type lines, so missingness itself can carry information.
  • Low CTR has many causes. Changes to the search results page, intent mismatch or AI answers can reduce clicks, not only a weak title or description.
  • Concentrated queue. One client supplies about 56% of the top 100.
  • Weak model signal. The Decision Tree did not reliably beat a simple baseline, so no model-based claims are made.
  • First-pass rules. Reason-code thresholds are starting points. A high-ranked page can land on “monitor,” and thresholds should be tuned with editors.

6. Ranked recommendations

These are review priorities, not guaranteed improvements.

  1. Review the top-ranked pages first: high impressions, positions 4–10, CTR far below the group’s typical CTR. The top 100 pages hold about 26% of all estimated missed clicks (top 500: 54%; top 1,000: 68%).
  2. Check title, meta description and intent match for pages flagged as page-one with CTR below half of expected.
  3. Review the on-page experience for pages flagged with weak engagement.
  4. Monitor, don’t act, on pages with fewer than 10 sessions until there is more evidence.
  5. Balance reviews across clients so one client does not dominate the queue, and tune the reason-code thresholds with the editors who use it.

7. Reproducibility

Everything in this paper comes from one notebook that runs top to bottom. The dataset itself is not committed to the repository, so the notebook fetches it from the public FlyRank starter repository.
